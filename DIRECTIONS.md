# Fleet Dice — where it can go from here

Written after v07, when the base finally held together. Everything here is built
on things we measured rather than guessed, so each idea comes with the number
that makes it plausible and the number that would break it.

---

## What the base actually gives us

Worth stating plainly, because the good directions all lean on one of these.

**Straights are the engine.** They're 49% of all damage in a match, and their
share climbs from 17% in round one to 56% by round ten. Anything that touches
straights is touching the middle of the game.

**Damage is a difference, not a total.** `your attack − their shields`. That's
why an 8-point bonus in round two triples your damage while the same 8 points in
round ten adds a third. Small numbers hit hard in this game, which means new
mechanics can be modest and still feel enormous.

**Fuel is the only thing stopping bigger-is-better.** Nine d10s score literally
zero because none of them launch. That single rule is load-bearing; any new
mechanic that hands out free Energy is quietly attacking it.

**Shields are currently decorative.** 5.6 average against 28 attack. This is the
biggest hole in the game and also the biggest opportunity — a whole path to
victory is sitting there unbuilt.

**Multipliers break everything, flat bonuses don't.** A ×2 panel tested 40% ahead
early and 69% ahead by mid-game. Per-die bonuses are safe because they stop at
your slot count. This is the sharpest design rule we have: **bonuses may scale
with how many ships you own, never with how much damage you already deal.**

**Early rounds matter more than they look.** The halftime leader wins 64% of
matches. Shortening the game to 8 rounds *lowers* that to 58%, which is why we
kept ten.

## Five paths to victory

A path needs three things to be real: an engine that generates advantage, a cost
that hurts, and a counter that beats it. Right now the game has one path — build
attack, chase straights. Here are five, arranged so each has a predator.

### 1. The Formation fleet — many small ships

Eight cheap dice, chasing long straights. Small dice pay the most Energy per
face, so this build is rich, and lots of dice means lots of numbers to make a run
of six.

**Cost:** low raw attack. **Counter:** anything that kills ships, since losing two
dice can break a run of six down to a run of three. This is what makes the
Assassin path below matter.

### 2. The Wall — the path that doesn't exist yet

The fix shields need isn't a bigger number, it's **a way for unused defense to
matter.** Three options, in the order I'd try them:

**Charge.** Surplus shields bank as Charge. Spend Charge on attack later at 2:1.
A defensive round becomes a stored offensive round, so blocking is never wasted
and the Wall player is a coiled spring. This also fixes the wasted-shield problem
that has been distorting every panel we priced.

**Counterfire.** Every 3 surplus shields reflect 1 damage back. Now a big attack
into a big wall *hurts the attacker*, which is the first genuine punish in the
game and makes reading your opponent worth doing.

**Bastion.** Surplus shields carry into next round as a standing wall. Simplest,
but it can snowball into stalemate, so it needs a cap.

**Counter to the Wall:** your pierce idea. Straights ignore shields. It changes
nothing today (133 damage either way, because there's no wall to pierce) but it
becomes the perfect counter the moment the Wall is real. Park it until then —
that's exactly the right order.

### 3. The Reactor — win the late game

All-in on Energy: cheap dice with low faces, banked Energy, buy the biggest
possible fleet by round seven and out-scale everyone.

**Cost:** you lose rounds one through five. With the halftime leader winning 64%,
that's a genuine gamble rather than a free ride. **Counter:** anyone who rushes
damage early, and the fact that the deficit compounds.

### 4. The Assassin — take their ships away

The Fire Control ladder from the older engine, which we built and tested and
never brought over: **Scar** an upgraded face back to factory, **Stun** a ship for
a round, **Breach** it permanently. Gated behind both a tech investment and
rolling four of a kind.

**Cost:** every Energy spent on Fire Control is a ship you didn't buy. **Counter:**
redundancy — the Formation fleet has eight ships and shrugs; the Glass Cannon has
three and dies.

This is the path that produces the screenshots. "He breached my d10 on round
eight" is a story.

### 5. The Glass Cannon — three enormous ships

Two d10s and a d8, all fuel, all attack. Highest ceiling in the game since a d10
straight of five pays 25.

**Cost:** fuel burn, and only three dice means the odds of a long straight
collapse. **Counter:** the Assassin, brutally — lose one ship and a third of your
fleet is gone.

**The web:** Formation feeds the Assassin's counter, Assassin eats the Glass
Cannon, Glass Cannon out-damages the Wall, Wall absorbs Formation, Reactor beats
anyone who over-invests early and loses to anyone who rushes. Not a clean
rock-paper-scissors, which is better — it's a web with soft edges.

## Where the bluffing lives

You asked for the feeling of one team pretending to swing big while they were
quietly buying ships. The good news is the game already has the ingredient.

### Hidden Energy, visible fleet

Your eight dice are public after every reveal. **Your Energy bank is not.** So a
player who hasn't bought anything for three rounds is a loaded gun and nobody
knows the calibre. That read — *he's saving for something, do I strike now or
brace* — is free, it already works, and it's the whole reason to keep Energy
secret. Don't ever show it.

### Secret Directives

The strongest single idea in this document. Each player draws **one hidden
objective** at the start, worth a real chunk of score:

- End with six or more ships
- Land a straight of six at any point
- Block 40 damage across the match
- Own two d10s at the end
- Deal 25+ in a single round
- Never scrap a ship

Suddenly your strange behaviour has a reason nobody can see. Why did he buy three
d4s on round eight when he was winning? Why is she hoarding? Directives create
paths *and* bluffs *and* table talk in one mechanic, and they're how Scythe and
Terraforming Mars make every game feel different. They also let a losing player
have something to play for in round nine, which matters enormously in a game
friends play over a week.

### One Trump each

Every player gets **one single-use Overcharge** for the whole match, chosen at
setup from a few options:

| Trump | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **Alpha Strike** | Add your entire Energy bank to attack this round |
| **Bastion** | Double your shields this round |
| **Requisition** | Buy three ships immediately, ignoring slot limits for a round |
| **Jam** | One named opponent's straight doesn't count this round |

One use, ten rounds, and everyone can see you still have it. The whole
meta-game becomes *when*. That's the super-weapon moment you described, and
"he'd been sitting on Jam for eight rounds waiting for my run of six" is exactly
the message someone screenshots.

**Jam is the important one** — it's the first mechanic that reaches across and
touches someone else's roll, which is what makes an opponent feel like an
opponent rather than a scoreboard.

## Two versus two

Everything above works in 1v1. This is what only exists with a partner, and the
first one is the reason to build teams at all.

### Joint straights

**Your dice and your teammate's dice combine into one straight.** You have 3-4-5,
they have 6-7, and the team scores a straight of five.

This is the killer mechanic and it's not close. It takes the system that's
already 49% of the game and makes it a conversation. "I've got 3, 4 and 5 locked
— can anyone cover a 6?" is a text message someone sends before the deadline, and
that text *is* the game. It needs no new numbers, no new icons, and no
explanation beyond one sentence.

The tension is real too: committing a die to the joint straight means it isn't in
yours, so you can be let down. A teammate who promises a 6 and rolls a 2 has
cost you a run of five.

### The specialist roles

Because straights need variety and the fuel economy needs small ships, a team can
split jobs in ways a solo player can't:

- **The Wall** rolls almost no attack and screens their teammates' damage
- **The Refinery** runs cheap dice, banks Energy, and gifts it (at a tax, so it
  isn't a free pool)
- **The Gunner** runs the Glass Cannon everyone else is feeding
- **The Spotter** runs Interceptors, rolls the Locks, and hands them to whoever
  bought the Fire Control levels

That last one is my favourite: two players who each can't Breach alone doing it
together.

### Focus fire

If two teammates target the same opponent, their damage combines **before** that
opponent's shields subtract, rather than each being reduced separately. Focusing
overwhelms a wall; spreading lets everyone block. It makes "who do we hit" the
team's most important shared decision each round, and it's the thing that stops a
3v3 feeling like three parallel duels.

### Six friends, one deadline

The format you described — everyone locks, weapons cross the galaxy, you open the
app later to the carnage — has a specific consequence worth designing for:
**the waiting is the social layer.** People will text during it. So the game
should give them things to text about:

- Show who has locked and who hasn't, but never what they locked
- Show every player's fleet grid, so people can scout and gossip
- Show who still holds their Trump
- Make the reveal screen worth screenshotting: dice flying in, straights firing
  in sequence, shields visibly failing, and a single big number at the end

## Would more ships open new paths?

You asked to leave this open. Worth flagging what the numbers say: **more slots
makes bigger dice worse, not better.** We measured it — quality only beats
quantity when slots are scarce. At five slots, buying big scored 153 against 141
for cheap spam; at nine slots the gap nearly closed.

So slot count is a dial that tilts the whole game:

| Slots | What it favours |
| --- | --- |
| 5–6 | Quality. Every purchase is a replacement decision. Glass Cannon viable. |
| 8 | Current. Balanced, mild lean toward quantity. |
| 10–12 | Quantity, long straights, Formation dominant. Assassin gets much stronger. |

That makes fleet size a great **variant** rather than a fix: a "Skirmish" at six
slots plays like a knife fight, an "Armada" at twelve plays like a fleet action
where losing ships genuinely hurts. Same rules, different game. That's cheap
replayability once the base is solid.

## What modern games are worth stealing from, and what specifically

- **Galaxy Trucker** — build in private, then watch the carnage together. This is
  structurally the closest game to yours and its whole appeal is the reveal.
  Worth studying how it makes failure funny rather than annoying.
- **7 Wonders / Roll for the Galaxy** — everyone acts simultaneously, nobody
  waits. Already how yours works; both prove it scales to six players.
- **Scythe / Terraforming Mars** — hidden objectives creating divergent paths.
  Directly the Secret Directives idea.
- **Air Land & Sea / Battle Line** — bluffing through committed, hidden choices.
  Where the Trump timing idea comes from.
- **Quacks of Quedlinburg** — the push-your-luck ache. Your nudge decision is a
  quiet version of it; there may be room for a louder one.
- **Root / Spirit Island** — asymmetric factions with different engines. If ship
  colours ever come back, this is the model: not "more attack" but *a different
  way to generate advantage*.
- **Dice Throne** — already borrowed the upgradeable-ability-card spine.
- **Dominion / Star Realms** — already borrowed the buy-and-improve spine.

## The order I'd actually build these

Grounded in what's broken versus what's merely missing.

1. **Fix shields with Charge.** It's the biggest hole, it unlocks a whole path,
   and it makes every defensive panel we've priced honest.
2. **Then pierce.** Straights ignore shields. Meaningless today, excellent the
   moment the Wall exists.
3. **Secret Directives.** Cheapest idea with the largest effect on replay value
   and table talk. Pure addition, breaks nothing.
4. **Trumps, especially Jam.** First mechanic that touches an opponent's roll.
5. **Bring Fire Control over** from the old engine. It's already written and
   tested; it just needs porting into the simple build.
6. **Then multiplayer**, and lead with joint straights.
7. **Slot-count variants** last, as replayability once everything else is stable.

Steps 1 and 2 together are probably one evening's work and they'd change the
feel of the game more than anything else on this list.

## The rules that keep us honest

Every one of these was learned the hard way in this project. Worth keeping
somewhere we'll see them.

1. **Nothing multiplies.** Bonuses may scale with ships owned, never with damage
   dealt.
2. **A bonus that fires most rounds isn't a bonus.** At 75% frequency it's just
   base attack with extra steps.
3. **Price by measured value, not by size.** Two d4s beat one d6 at the obvious
   prices, which made saving up mathematically wrong.
4. **Defence needs a lasting effect** or nobody builds it, because damage both
   scores and compounds while shields evaporate.
5. **Simulate before believing.** Being wrong about rerolls, about d10 straights
   being rare, and about shorter games helping — none of those were guessable.
6. **Simple beats clever.** The version that stalled on "I don't know what all
   this means" had more mechanics than the one that works.
